\begin{abstract}
Timber strength classes are assigned on the basis of a strength property, typically compressive or bending strength, and each class carries a tabulated modulus of elasticity that the designer is required to adopt. This study examines whether that tabulated stiffness is representative of the species actually assigned to the class, and what the consequences are for the design of short-span round-timber bridges on low-volume roads, where the serviceability deflection limit is frequently the governing criterion. Mechanical properties measured on 40 Brazilian tropical hardwood species were assigned to the strength classes of ABNT NBR 7190-3 and the resulting representation error was quantified. The bending modulus of elasticity scatters by up to \tofill{74}\% within a single class, whereas the step between adjacent classes is only \tofill{14} to \tofill{21}\%, so that within-class scatter exceeds the class resolution by a factor of about four in the two classes holding most of the population. \tofill{58}\% of the species fall in a different class when assigned by measured stiffness rather than by characteristic compressive strength, and \tofill{35}\% are less stiff than their class prescribes, making class-based design unconservative for the deflection limit state. The structural consequence was evaluated by robust multiobjective optimisation of the superstructure over a range of theoretical spans, using a fully parametric model coupled to the NSGA-II algorithm, with each species designed twice: once with its measured properties and once with the tabulated properties of its assigned class. \tofill{[A PREENCHER: uma ou duas frases com o resultado estrutural -- faixa do erro de volume de madeira devido ao enquadramento, e como ele varia com o vão à medida que o critério governante migra da resistência para a rigidez]}. The results indicate that the limitation is not the number of classes but the choice of indexing property, and that stiffness-relevant verification of the assigned class is warranted whenever serviceability governs.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords}: timber bridges; tropical hardwoods; strength classes; modulus of elasticity; serviceability limit state; multiobjective optimisation; NSGA-II.
